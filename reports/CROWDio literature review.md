# Phone crowds force partition planners to price switching

*A literature review for the CROWDio project (Foreman orchestrator, Android mobile worker, Python SDK, and reconfiguration-aware partition planner). Prepared for Lakshani Manamperi, University of Moratuwa. Literature coverage to November 2026.*

**Abstract.** Mobile crowd computing (MCC) has matured from MapReduce prototypes on phones into dependency-aware work-stealing frameworks, and collaborative DNN inference has matured from one-cut device–cloud splits into multi-device pipelines for transformers and large language models. The two lines have barely met. Across the 21 papers in the project's Zotero library and roughly 180 further works from Google Scholar, arXiv and publisher indexes, almost every partitioning and pipeline system either replicates the full model on every node, so that changing the cut is free (Neurosurgeon, Edgent, DNN-Scissor, Luger), or fixes the plan once at deployment (EdgeShard, PipeEdge, Galaxy, AMP4EC). The only phone-crowd inference system with churn handling, CrowdNet, sidesteps repartitioning by retraining a modular network and accepting an accuracy loss. CROWDio's distinctive claim, that on single-residency Android workers a plan change costs tens of seconds (about 48 s cold and 6 s warm in its own published measurements) and must therefore be priced inside the cut-and-placement search and gated by a break-even, hysteresis and epoch controller, is consistent with this gap but is *not* unprecedented in its parts. Huang, Zhang and Xu (arXiv:2410.01857) already motivate a switching penalty as "reloading and setting up the DNN models in the device memory"; Djuhera, Koch and Binotto already use EWMA triggers with a 30 s cooldown "to prevent thrashing"; IONN already prices model upload inside the partition graph; NEUKONFIG already measures repartitioning downtime; and CROWDio's own preprints (arXiv:2604.19363 and 2605.20723) already publish the 48 s/6 s numbers, the four-tier residency scheduler and the checkpointing SDK. What remains novel is the conjunction: a measured, decomposed, residency-dependent switch cost (unload, conditional fetch and write, load, session initialisation) embedded in an exhaustive or Pareto-DP cut × placement search for a three-or-more-stage pipeline on real volunteer phones; a voluntary-switch controller combining margin, persistence, cooldown, a switch cap and refusal on stale telemetry; drain-versus-resume epoch semantics; and phone-side standby prefetch, peer shard fetch and direct phone-to-phone activations. The review also documents that several references in the project's workshop paper are misattributed or unverifiable (Appendix A), and that two Zotero items (Choudhary et al. 2025 and AMP4EC) carry internal inconsistencies that limit their use as quantitative evidence.

## 1. Introduction and scope: three subsystems, two preprints, one unpublished planner

CROWDio is a mobile crowd computing platform developed at the University of Moratuwa. It has three parts. The backend "Foreman" is a FastAPI service with a WebSocket hub and a SQLite job store that manages jobs and pipeline DAGs, schedules work with pluggable rankers, checkpoints and resumes tasks, and can be exercised in trace-driven simulators against a virtual device fleet. The Android mobile worker is a Kotlin foreground service hosting Python through Chaquopy and running ONNX Runtime or TFLite; it loads DNN partitions just in time, keeps a single partition resident, ships tensors as compressed self-describing payloads and reports telemetry including battery and temperature. The Python SDK exposes map and pipeline primitives, declarative checkpointing, a verified shard exporter with a manifest, and declarative model jobs. On the current `feature/reconfiguration-aware-planning` branch, a fourth component, the reconfiguration-aware partition planner, chooses cut points and placements from EWMA telemetry, prices plan changes under single residency, and switches only when a break-even rule with hysteresis says the gain outweighs the cost.

A literature review for CROWDio must separate what the project has already published from what exists only in code, because two preprints now pre-empt part of the novelty space. The first, *CROWDio: A Practical Mobile Crowd Computing Framework with Developer-Oriented Design, Adaptive Scheduling, and Fault Resilience* [1], describes the Coordinator–Worker design, a declarative SDK in which a single function annotation turns sequential code into a distributed workload, tiered checkpointing (base, delta and compacted snapshots, with compaction every k = 50 deltas by default) and an entropy-weighted MCDM scheduler (EDAS, ARAS, MABAC, with WRR and FIFO baselines) driven by live CPU, memory, battery, latency and thermal telemetry. On six heterogeneous Android phones it reports up to **56.9% lower execution time than round-robin**, checkpoint overhead bounded at **2–3 s per task**, and a Jain fairness index of 0.889 [1]. It explicitly does not cover bandwidth-aware scheduling, simulators, Chaquopy, model-shard export or framework-aware deltas [1]. The second, *Memory-Efficient Partitioned DNN Inference on Resource-Constrained Android Crowds* [2], accepted at the ICML 2026 Workshop on Machine Learning for the Global South, describes the DNN pipeline subsystem: just-in-time (JIT) shard loading, single-partition residency, a four-tier affinity scheduler (resident, cached on disk, idle, evict), zlib tensor transport and 1:1 streaming dependencies, evaluated on DistilBERT/SST-2 split into three static cells across five handsets with 3.3–6 GB of RAM. It reports **43 ± 2 MB peak RSS per device, 50 ± 3 mAh per run, a 34% lower pipeline latency for streaming than for barrier mode (18.4 s vs 27.9 s), a 48 ± 4 s cold load and a 6 ± 1 s warm load** [2]. ## 2. Mobile crowd and volunteer computing: from MapReduce prototypes to dependency-aware work stealing

Mobile crowd computing treats publicly owned smartphones as an opportunistic, dynamic grid. Pramanik, Pal and Choudhury's 95-page survey in the *Journal of Supercomputing* is the canonical framing reference: it defines MCC, gives architecture models and requirements, and contrasts MCC with desktop grids, clusters, mobile grids, ad hoc mobile clouds and crowdsourcing [3]. An earlier survey by Fernando, Loke and Rahayu identified the lack of developer-facing abstractions as a persistent gap in mobile cloud computing [4], a gap the CROWDio SDK targets directly.

The first generation of systems ported cluster abstractions to phones. Hyrax ran MapReduce on mobile devices [5] and Misco provided a MapReduce framework for mobile systems [6]. Honeybee introduced work stealing as the load-balancing primitive for independent jobs across phones that join and leave unpredictably [7]; its journal successor reported up to 4× speed-up with seven devices and up to 71% energy savings with eight on an Android prototype, and tolerated churn by re-stealing lost work rather than checkpointing [8]. CWC took the opposite, centralised route: it scheduled jobs only on phones that were charging, profiled tasks and assigned small jobs with awareness of bandwidth [9], [10]. Among classic systems, CWC is the closest precedent to CROWDio's centralised, telemetry- and bandwidth-aware Foreman. Serendipity distributed DAG-structured jobs over intermittently connected phones using opportunistic contacts [11], and the mClouds vision paper argued for clouds of mobile devices [12]. In desktop volunteer computing, BOINC remains the reference middleware, handling heterogeneity and churn largely through redundancy and pre-registered native applications [13].

The Deakin/Queensland Honeybee line is CROWDio's most active contemporary peer. Honeybee-T added task-dependency-based work stealing [14], and Honeybee-Tx (*Future Internet*, 2025) added capability-aware job selection, static dependency-aware stealing and staged result transfers; abstract-level reports give 2.06× speed-up, 51.4% lower energy, 71.6% memory savings and 42% less network traffic than dependency-oblivious stealing on video workloads, and up to 4.72× speed-up with 63% lower per-device energy for human-activity recognition [15]. The full text of Honeybee-Tx was inaccessible (the publisher returned HTTP 403), so its fault-tolerance mechanisms are unverified.

Honeybee-DR, in the Zotero library, is the newest member and the most direct comparator for CROWDio's dynamic-DAG and reliability concerns [16]. It is a delegator-centric, infrastructure-free framework over Wi-Fi Direct in a star topology: a single delegator owns the job pool and the DAG, while workers are stateless, never see the DAG, and request work by "steal requests". Its first contribution is runtime DAG growth through an application callback invoked on every result, which selects one of three patterns (data-dependent branching, iterative refinement, conditional cascade) and adds jobs and edges atomically at O(|J_new| + |E_new|) cost, with a clean separation of mechanism (the middleware) from policy (the application). Stealing proceeds in three phases: dependency resolution, capability filtering against the requesting worker's profile, and adaptive chunking sized from historical throughput and current latency. A battery policy aligned with Android Battery Saver restricts heavy branches below 30% and disables discovery below 20%. Its second contribution is adaptive trap-job validation: a per-worker reliability score updated asymmetrically (η = 0.1) determines how many trap jobs (up to five) are inserted into each chunk. On five phones (a OnePlus 5T delegator, a Pixel 7 and three Pixel 6 Pros) running multi-camera video through YOLOv4-tiny, the static mode cuts makespan from 468 s to 227 s and peak delegator memory from 2234 MB to 635 MB against baselines, and the dynamic mode lowers processing time by about 17%, energy by 21% and peak memory by 30.5% relative to the static mode, at about 5% more network traffic. Adaptive traps reach 99.5% accuracy against rogue workers with up to 80.5% less overhead than three-way majority voting [16]. The paper is candid about its limits: the delegator is a single point of failure, the DAG lives only in memory, the two contributions are never evaluated jointly, mobility and Doze are out of scope, dropped workers are not reconnected, and there are no live baselines against other dynamic-DAG systems; all gains are against its own static mode [16].

The contrast with CROWDio is structural. Honeybee-DR is pull-based and serverless; CROWDio is push-scheduled from a persistent server-side job store. Honeybee-DR absorbs worker loss by reissuing stolen jobs from scratch; CROWDio checkpoints and resumes. Honeybee-DR folds latency only into chunk size; CROWDio's current branch models transfer time explicitly. And Honeybee-DR has no notion of DNN partition placement or switching cost. Conversely, CROWDio has no result-validation mechanism, and Honeybee-DR's asymmetric reliability score is a natural additional MCDM criterion; its mechanism/policy split for DAG growth is a template for conditional pipeline stages. A precursor from CROWDio's own group, Kiridana et al.'s mobile-agent work-stealing system (MERCon 2024), belongs to the same work-stealing family [17].

A parallel line from Hirsch, Mateos and Zunino studies "dew computing" with smartphone clusters, evaluating scheduling heuristics with real battery traces and small clusters running TensorFlow object recognition [18], ranking devices by last-reported battery level combined with performance scores [19]. Security of Android volunteer computing has been treated mainly as a malware problem [20], and Diskin et al. observe that volunteer computing has had few successes in machine learning because of communication demands [21]. Taken together, the MCC literature shows that the most recent peer-reviewed frameworks are still decentralised work stealing over static or monotonically growing dependencies, without explicit per-link bandwidth models, cached-artifact accounting or DNN placement. CROWDio's centralised, telemetry-driven, cost-model-based dispatcher occupies a distinct point in this design space.

## 3. Backend orchestration: from offline MCDM rankings to transfer-time-aware dispatch

### 3.1 Multi-criteria resource selection has been compared offline but rarely run online

The direct precedent for CROWDio's scheduler is Pramanik et al.'s comparison of five MCDM methods (EDAS, ARAS, MABAC, COPRAS, MARCOS) with entropy weights for resource selection in MCC, published in *Symmetry* in 2021, which ran the methods on a laptop and a phone and performed sensitivity and rank-order analysis against a dataset of static smartphone specifications [22]. The method originals are EDAS [23] (published in 2015, not 2017 as listed in [2]), ARAS [24], MABAC [25] and Shannon entropy [26]. A follow-up by Pramanik and Biswas benchmarks the energy efficiency of seven scheduling algorithms, including PSO, GA, Min-Min and Max-Min, for MCC [27]. Stan et al. show that conventional policies degrade on low-capacity edge nodes [28], and Saleh and Shastry schedule globally on observed per-device performance in volunteer computing [29]. Work on availability prediction, such as Javadi, Matawie and Anderson's Markovian models of volunteer host availability [30], points to a capability CROWDio lacks: it reacts to churn through checkpoint/resume rather than predicting it.

CROWDio's published contribution [1] is to move this MCDM line from offline ranking to an online dispatcher fed by live telemetry, including RTT and thermal state, which the MCC MCDM literature reviewed here does not use as criteria (no exhaustive search was made to prove the absence). The closest analogue outside MCC is IPA, the network-aware Kubernetes scheduler of Caminero and Muñoz-Mansilla in the Zotero library [31]. IPA probes each node's bandwidth and RTT with iperf every 30 s, then filters nodes whose historical mean completion time for the pod type exceeds the job's deadline and ranks the rest by a weighted sum of min–max-normalised bandwidth, RTT, CPU, memory and same-type pod count, rejecting jobs that no node can meet. On a one-master, four-worker Kind cluster running 200 bandwidth-heavy speedtest jobs, the IPA-E configuration (equal weight on bandwidth and pod count) was the only one to meet 100% of accepted jobs' deadlines at 120 s, beating CA-Net by more than 30%, while the network-only configuration IPA-N missed about 15% of deadlines [31]. The paper's lesson, that network status alone is insufficient and must be combined with load and completion-time prediction, supports CROWDio's combination of network cost with compute and residency. Its limitations are equally instructive: a network view up to 30 s stale, random placement for the first ten jobs, a plain per-(pod, node) mean estimator, one emulated cluster on a single host and one job type [31]. Structurally, IPA's rank step is CROWDio's MCDM ranking with hand-set rather than entropy-derived weights; CROWDio's EWMA telemetry is more responsive than IPA's historical mean; and IPA's deadline-based admission control is a feature CROWDio could adopt.

### 3.2 Network-aware scheduling: planning data and compute together beats placing tasks over fixed data

CROWDio's current branch estimates per-batch completion time as RTT plus uncached bytes over downlink plus compute plus result over uplink, shares a Wi-Fi cell among concurrent transfers by max-min fairness, and maps batches with Sufferage. None of this appears in either preprint (Table 1). Its lineage is the heterogeneous-mapping family: Maheswaran et al. introduced Sufferage and compared it with Min-Min and MCT [32], Braun et al. compared eleven static mapping heuristics and found Min-Min and GA strong [33], and HEFT/CPOP extended list scheduling to DAGs on heterogeneous processors [34]. The closest precedent found for "Sufferage plus bandwidth" at the edge is a 2022 KSII paper that selects the server with the highest bandwidth efficiency across multiple edge servers [35].

Jalaparti et al.'s Corral (SIGCOMM 2015), in the Zotero library, is the most important conceptual reference for the bandwidth-aware scheduler and, indirectly, for the planner [36]. Corral exploits the predictability of recurring data-parallel jobs, whose input size it predicts within 6.5% average error, to plan offline which set of racks each job's input data and tasks should occupy, and with what priority. Each job has a latency response function L_j(r) over the number of racks, composed of map, shuffle and reduce terms with a data-imbalance penalty; a provisioning heuristic repeatedly adds a rack to the current longest job, and a widest-job-first extension of LPT orders jobs, coming within 3% (batch) and 15% (online) of an LP lower bound at O(J²R²) cost. On a 210-machine YARN cluster with a 5:1 oversubscribed core, Corral cut batch makespan by 10–33%, median online job completion time by 30–56% and cross-rack data by 20–90%, and its gains survived 50% input-size prediction error at 25–35% [36]. In simulation, Corral over TCP beat the YARN capacity scheduler combined with the Varys flow scheduler by about 45% at the median, showing that co-planning placement is orthogonal to flow scheduling. Its limitations are its reliance on recurring jobs, fixed allocations without preemption, and latency models assuming uniform per-stage work [36].

Corral maps onto CROWDio at several points. Its "place input data where compute will run" is the analogue of CROWDio's prefetch and peer fetch of model shards to planned workers, and of the "args + code + model not yet on worker" term in the bandwidth-aware cost. L_j(r) is the analogue of CROWDio's per-batch estimate, and Corral's simulated TCP uses the same max-min fair sharing CROWDio applies to a shared Wi-Fi medium. The differences are just as clear: Corral plans racks with a stable topology and an oversubscribed core, whereas CROWDio's bottleneck is a single shared access medium among volatile phones; Corral plans once and stays fixed, whereas CROWDio replans by epochs; and Corral never pays to move or reload a model, which is precisely the cost CROWDio's planner prices. Corral's robustness tests (prediction error, arrival jitter) are a ready template for ablating CROWDio's planner against EWMA mis-estimation.

Corral sits within a wider data-locality literature. Iridium places data and tasks for geo-distributed analytics [37], Tetrium plans across multiple resources in wide-area analytics [38], Sparrow provides distributed low-latency scheduling [39], and delay scheduling trades a small wait for locality [40]. Ray's object-locality-aware scheduling is a design-philosophy precedent for CROWDio, though not built for mobile connectivity [41]. CROWDio's "uncached artifact" term is effectively code and data locality applied to phones whose downlink is the bottleneck. At the mobile edge, Cotter, Castiñeiras and Cionca measure bandwidth with an initial iperf3 baseline and then periodic 10-packet ping probes smoothed by an EWMA with α = 0.3, typically every 30 s; their Raspberry Pi testbed shows that more frequent probing adds congestion and that 75%-duty background traffic cuts frame completion by 18% [42]. That probe-overhead trade-off justifies CROWDio's mix of passive (piggy-backed) and active probing, which should be evaluated against probe-only baselines. ENTS jointly schedules compute and network resources across collaborating edge nodes [43]. No MCC framework found combines artifact-cache-aware transfer cost, shared-Wi-Fi fair share and Sufferage.

### 3.3 DAG and pipeline scheduling: runtime support on phones is rare

DAG scheduling at the edge is active but mostly simulation-based and DRL- or list-scheduling-driven. IBDASH orchestrates DAG applications over mixed commercial and personally owned edge devices, accounting for memory, bandwidth and device failure probabilities [44]; a 2024 arXiv paper schedules DAG applications across sporadically available personal devices and commercial edge nodes with static partitioning [45]; and a 2026 preprint argues that HEFT-style schedulers treat DAG nodes as indivisible and splits operators across a phone's CPU and GPU [46]. Serendipity remains the classic precedent for DAG jobs on intermittently connected phones [11]. Among MCC frameworks, Honeybee-Tx handles dependencies statically [15] and Honeybee-DR grows the DAG monotonically at runtime [16]. CROWDio's pipelines are declared statically through the SDK but distinguish streaming 1:1 edges from barrier 1:N edges with upstream result injection, which corresponds to pipelined versus bulk-synchronous dependencies; its published 34% latency reduction for streaming over barrier [2] is a same-platform ablation of exactly this distinction. The defensible claim relative to this literature is practical runtime support on real phones rather than a new DAG algorithm.

### 3.4 Fault tolerance: delta checkpointing exists, but not for Python on phones

Delta checkpointing is established in two adjacent settings. For container migration at the edge, Ngo et al. reduced migrated state to about 4% and 0.9% of a full checkpoint for two services, with 30–40% lower downtime [47]. For DL training, CheckFreq tunes checkpoint frequency and splits snapshot from persist [48], and Check-N-Run uses differential checkpoints plus quantisation [49]. Smartphone-specific checkpointing work is older and trigger-based; Tuli and Kumar's survey names low wireless bandwidth, lack of stable storage and battery as the core constraints [50], which supports CROWDio's decision to store checkpoints on the server side. Transparent system-level checkpointing (DMTCP [51], CRIU [52]) requires kernel features or privileges unavailable to ordinary Android apps. Straggler mitigation (Mantri [53], Dolly [54], LATE [55]) offers speculative replication, which CROWDio lacks and which would close a gap relative to BOINC-style redundancy.

Against this background, CROWDio's published tiered scheme, with source-level instrumentation that adds recovery at function entry, skips completed loop iterations and persists state after mutations, bounded at 2–3 s per task [1], is novel in its setting (Python task code on Android with orchestrator-side storage and cross-device resume after a WebSocket disconnect) rather than in delta checkpointing as such. The framework-aware deltas for PyTorch, TensorFlow and NumPy objects are codebase-only. In the DNN pipeline, the relevant comparison is with Honeybee-DR's stateless reissue [16], Petals' replay of stored inputs to rebuild attention state [56], and the forced-switch resume from a retained stage-boundary activation in CROWDio's planner (codebase-only), discussed in Section 6.

### 3.5 Evaluation by simulation: generic simulators reimplement the policy

CloudSim-derived simulators dominate edge evaluation: CloudSim [57], iFogSim [58], iFogSim2 with mobility and microservices [59], and EdgeCloudSim with MEC mobility and cellular models [60]; SimGrid offers validated flow-level network models [61], and YAFS is a Python/SimPy alternative [62]. A 2026 source-code analysis finds native fault-tolerance coverage in these simulators weak [63]. Smartphone-specific trace-driven simulation exists in the dew-computing line, which models battery behaviour from real-device traces [19]. Zotero papers show the evaluation spectrum: Corral used both a 210-machine testbed and a 2000-machine simulator [36], IPA ran on an emulated Kind cluster [31], DDS was simulation-only [64], Kafetzis et al. used a custom discrete-event simulator [65], and Koch et al. compiled "expected" results from prior studies without any experiment [66]. CROWDio's codebase-only simulator and virtual fleet replay the production Python scheduler objects against virtual devices with telemetry, RTT and bandwidth traces. The methodological claim, "same code in simulation and production", is stronger than reimplementing a policy in a Java simulator, but it should be calibrated against real-phone runs and ideally cross-checked against SimGrid-style network models for the shared Wi-Fi fair-share assumption. No public 2023–2026 smartphone fleet trace dataset (CPU, bandwidth, availability) was found; available churn traces date from around 2013.

## 4. Collaborative and partitioned DNN inference: replication made repartitioning free

### 4.1 Device–edge split computing chooses one cut per query because both sides hold the model

Kang et al.'s Neurosurgeon (ASPLOS 2017), in the Zotero library, founded layer-granularity mobile–cloud partitioning [67]. It builds per-layer-type regression models of latency and power (logarithmic or linear in GFLOPS) during a one-time profiling per hardware pair, then at runtime chooses the single split point that minimises latency or mobile energy given current uplink bandwidth and server load, the latter obtained by periodically pinging the server. Its latency model for split j is the sum of mobile compute up to j, cloud compute after j, and upload of layer j's output at the current bandwidth; it has no result-return, model-load or queueing term. On a Jetson TK1 "mobile" and a Xeon/K40 server, across eight DNNs, three networks and CPU/GPU, it chose the optimal split in 44 of 48 configurations, reaching 98.5% of the optimal latency speedup, and reported **3.1× average (up to 40.7×) latency speedup and 59.5% average (up to 94.7%) mobile energy reduction** over cloud-only execution, with 1.5× average datacenter throughput. A motivating measurement shows that for AlexNet over 3G the status quo spends over 94% of its time transferring a 152 KB image [67]. Crucially, "both NSmobile and NSserver host complete DNN models", so the split can change per query at zero cost [67]. The paper has no limitations section; it concedes only mispredictions when candidate splits perform similarly, and it reports no load or switch cost.

Edgent (Li, Zhou and Chen, SIGCOMM MECOMM 2018), also in the library, jointly selects a device–edge split and a BranchyNet early-exit point to maximise accuracy under a latency deadline, by linear search over exits and splits in under 1 ms [68]. It is the only one of the seven partitioning papers in the library whose latency model includes a **model-loading term**, fitted as a linear regression on model size (y = 1.33·10⁻⁶x + 2.182 ms on the device and y = 4.49·10⁻⁶x + 842.136 ms on the edge server). On a Raspberry Pi 3 and a desktop PC with AlexNet/CIFAR-10 and bandwidth shaped from 50 kbps to 1.5 Mbps, higher bandwidth yields deeper exits, and at 200–300 ms deadlines Edgent meets the deadline where the other methods fail [68]. It is a six-page workshop prototype that defers energy to future work, replicates the full model on both sides, and contains a text inconsistency about which side runs the layers before the partition point. The journal version adds a dynamic-network configuration map [69]. Edgent is the closest early precedent for treating load time as a latency cost; it still does not price a *switch* or model residency, and its deadline-constrained objective parallels CROWDio's declarative `submit_job(model_id, objective, deadline)` (codebase-only).

The wider split-computing line follows the same pattern. DADS formulates DAG partitioning as a min-cut and switches between light- and heavy-load regimes [70]; JALAD jointly chooses cut and feature quantisation by ILP [71]; SPINN schedules split point and early exit under fluctuating network and server load [72]; and the Autodidactic Neurosurgeon learns the split online as a contextual bandit without profiling [73]. All assume the model is present on both ends. IONN is the exception and is discussed in Section 6 [74]. Matsubara, Levorato and Restuccia's survey canonises the head/tail vocabulary of split computing and early exiting [75].

Yang et al.'s DDS (arXiv 2023), in the library, extends split computing to many selfish devices sharing one MEC server [64]. Each device chooses a resource budget in a non-cooperative game with proportional price inflation and solves its DAG partition as a min-cut on a "latency graph". The authors prove a unique Nash equilibrium (and PPAD-hardness of exact computation) and give a decentralised momentum-gradient algorithm that converges to the equilibrium price 1.09 within 25–50 iterations for 100 devices. In simulation only (Xeon host, 5–10 Mbps, 10–20 GFLOPS devices, a 1.2 TFLOPS server, VGG11/ResNet/ViT), DDS is "about 1.25×" better than DADS, which degenerates to local-only execution at 100 devices; the metric wording (acceleration versus throughput) is inconsistent between abstract and introduction [64]. Two features matter for CROWDio. First, contention on a shared resource changes each tenant's optimal cut, which CROWDio meets when jobs compete for phones and addresses centrally rather than through a market. Second, DDS's Fig. 4 shows that per-device cost is non-convex and non-smooth exactly where the optimal partition switches, the analytic signature of the oscillation risk that CROWDio damps with margin, hysteresis and cooldown. DDS itself prices no switching cost, its "Resource Sniff" re-probing is a crude analogue of re-evaluation epochs, the server is assumed to hold every model, and device entry and exit are mentioned but not evaluated [64].

Lyu et al.'s DNN-Scissor (*MAKE*, 2025), in the library, argues that greedy per-frame cut selection is myopic because it ignores queue build-up for later frames [76]. An A2C agent on the local device chooses among a predefined set of valid DAG cuts (eight for YOLOv3, some cutting three parallel branches) from a state of local and offload queue lengths, estimated bandwidth, delay and pending data, with reward −w·data − delay or a drop penalty F = 10. Decisions take 1–2 ms. On a laptop plus Jetson TX2 over a congested campus network with 5146 frames of 1080p traffic video, YOLOv3 total delay falls 83% and system cost 67% against a Neurosurgeon-style greedy baseline [76]. Two caveats temper the result: the edge is *slower* than the laptop for ResNet-18 and AlexNet, and DNN-Scissor drops 68% *more* frames than edge-only on YOLOv3 despite the abstract's claim of "nearly eliminating frame drops". The model is deployed on both nodes, so per-frame switching is free; the authors list predefined splits, hard-coded penalties, offline DRL training and single-edge topology as limitations, naming "multi-device and multi-edge scenarios" as future work [76]. CROWDio shares DNN-Scissor's diagnosis that greedy per-decision choice is myopic, but answers with an explicit, interpretable cost comparison that includes switching cost, because under single residency a per-frame action change would cost 6–48 s against a frame interval of about one second.

### 4.2 Multi-device pipelines: heterogeneity-aware, but static and trusting

Multi-device partitioning began with CNNs on local clusters. MoDNN partitioned layers across WLAN-connected phones but moved large intermediate data [77]; DeepThings used fused tile partitioning of early convolutional layers with work stealing, reporting 1.7–3.5× speed-up on 2–6 devices with under 23 MB each [78]; AOFL adapted the degree of parallelism to heterogeneous devices [79]; DeepSlicing combined model and data partitioning for up to 5.79× latency reduction [80]; DistrEdge used deep RL to choose CNN splits across heterogeneous devices [81]; and CoEdge partitioned workload adaptively and exchanged only overlapping feature-map regions [82]. PICO split both the CNN DAG and the device set into pipeline stages, reaching 1.8–6.8× throughput on 2–8 Raspberry Pis, and criticised CoEdge's per-layer communication as too costly over wireless [83], an argument that favours CROWDio's coarse stage pipelines. None of these uses volunteer phones or handles churn.

Hu et al.'s arXiv preprint "Pipeline Parallelism for Inference on Heterogeneous Edge Computing" (system name EdgePipe), in the library, later published as PipeEdge at Euromicro DSD 2022 [84], is the cleanest prior statement of CROWDio's steady-state pipeline model. It assumes asynchronous communication with perfect overlap, so that the pipeline period is max(T_comp, T_comm) of the slowest stage, and finds a contiguous partition and device assignment by a dynamic program over (layers processed, used-device set, next device), with complexity O(2^D·L²·D²) reducible when devices fall into identical types; for ViT-Base on nine devices the DP takes 0.01 s versus 71 minutes for brute force. On MinnowBoard and RCC-VE Atom boards with emulated heterogeneity and 1 Gbps links, it reports 10.59× (ViT-Large) and 11.88× (ViT-Huge) speed-up on 16 devices over a single device, up to 4.16× over PipeDream's partitioner on heterogeneous clusters, and robustness down to about 30 Mbps [84]. It is fully static: no telemetry-driven replanning, no failure handling, no model-load or switching cost, and no phones. Importantly for the user's paper, the preprint contains **no "43%" figure, no "affinity-aware placement" and no measurement of inter-stage transfer reduction**, its first author is Hu (Zhao is third), and it was never published at INFOCOM (Appendix A).

Zhang et al.'s AMP4EC (arXiv 2025), in the library, illustrates the monitor–partition–schedule–deploy decomposition that CROWDio also follows [85]. A Docker-stats resource monitor feeds a partitioner that fills contiguous partitions to equal cumulative cost (kernel size × channels for convolutions, input × output for fully connected layers), a scheduler that ranks nodes by a fixed weighted score (0.2/0.2/0.1/0.5), and a deployer. On Docker containers on a single Mac with MobileNetV2, it reports 78.35% lower latency and 414.73% higher throughput than a single-container monolith [85]. The paper should be cited only as an example of the pattern, not as quantitative evidence, because of several internal inconsistencies: the throughput gain is "414%" in the abstract and "415%" in the introduction; the text claims caching reduces network bandwidth "to zero" while its own Table I lists 100 MB for both cached and uncached variants; memory "efficiency" is claimed for 1.625 MB versus a monolith's 0.625 MB, which is higher; one section reports 22–40 ms per profile while Table II reports 234.56–583.91 ms; and the partitioner is said to consider communication although its equation uses computation only [85]. Its own limitations concede that "partition boundaries are fixed after deployment", that all devices share one architecture and OS, and that device-offline events, the paper's motivation, were not tested [85]. AMP4EC's hand-tuned weights contrast with CROWDio's entropy-derived weights, and its fixed boundaries are exactly what CROWDio's planner relaxes.

Hao et al.'s *ACM Computing Surveys* article (2026), in the library, gives the field's most complete map [86]. It classifies architectures as one-to-one, multiple-to-one, one-to-multiple, multiple-to-multiple and peer-to-peer; offers a unified cost model of latency (compute + transmission + queueing), energy, monetary cost, accuracy and reliability, under constraints C1–C6 (partition feasibility, compute, deadline, bandwidth, accuracy, storage); classifies methods into six solution-space levels from single-cut linear search and min-cut through multi-layer offloading to tensor parallelism; and tabulates toolchains, datasets and hardware. Three observations from the survey define CROWDio's gap. Its unified model has **no switching or reload term**; its storage constraint C6 assumes that nodes "typically need to store the complete DNN model"; and its robustness challenge calls for "automating rapid re-partitioning in response to node overload or failure", noting that most reliability work relies on multi-replica strategies with significant overhead and pointing to NEUKONFIG [86]. Its hardware tables list Raspberry Pi and Jetson boards but no smartphones or ONNX Runtime Mobile, and its toolchain section catalogues profilers and bandwidth shapers but no artefact pipeline for exporting and verifying shards. In the survey's taxonomy, CROWDio sits in the peer-to-peer/one-to-multiple cells with multi-layer partitioning plus offloading under a central coordinator.

### 4.3 Transformers and LLMs at the edge: dynamic programs over resident layers

Vaswani et al.'s transformer (NIPS 2017), in the library, explains why layer-range cuts are natural for CROWDio's workload [87]. A transformer is a stack of N identical layers, each wrapped as LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)), and all sub-layers and embeddings output width d_model (512 in the base model, about 65 M parameters; 1024 in the big model, about 213 M). The activation crossing any layer boundary is therefore always an n × d_model tensor, and self-attention costs O(n²·d) per layer [87]. For an encoder-only model such as DistilBERT, cut choice is therefore driven by compute and memory balance rather than by activation size, except at the embedding and classifier ends; and the residual wrapping implies cuts must fall between whole layers. The paper itself contains a minor inconsistency (41.8 BLEU for EN-FR in the abstract versus 41.0 in the results section).

Zhang et al.'s EdgeShard (*IEEE IoT-J*, 2025), in the library under the key "EdgeLLM", performs offline, profile-once joint device selection and layer partitioning by dynamic programming [88]. Its latency DP minimises compute plus communication with a privacy constraint pinning layer 0 to the source node and a final token-return term; its throughput DP minimises the slowest device's max(t_comp, t_comm). On 12 Jetson AGX Orin, 2 Orin NX and one RTX 3090 server with Llama2-7B/13B/70B at full precision, it reaches 75.88 ms/token for 7B (about 1.85× faster than edge-solo and cloud–edge baselines) and 52.45 tokens/s (about 2.2×), and is the only method that fits 70B, which all baselines run out of memory on. A "No-Bubbles" variant reorders micro-batches so decoding starts early [88]. EdgeShard's "dynamic model loading" is only a profiling device for memory-limited nodes; at runtime it has no telemetry loop, no model-load cost, no re-partitioning trigger, no handling of device departure and no phones. It is nonetheless the closest prior work for CROWDio's memory filter plus DP over ordered device assignments, and its steady-state max(t_comp, t_comm) matches CROWDio's per-batch term (CROWDio additionally models the pipeline-fill term).

Galaxy pools trusted home devices using hybrid tensor and sequence parallelism planned offline once, achieving 1.3–2.5× lower latency on Jetson Nano testbeds for models from DistilBERT to OPT-XL [89]; Jupiter adds intra-sequence pipelining and speculative decoding for up to 26.1× lower latency [90]; TPI-LLM uses tensor parallelism with a sliding-window memory scheduler on laptops [91]; HexGen [92] and Helix [93] plan asymmetric or max-flow placements over heterogeneous GPUs; and a 2024 systematic review provides a taxonomy of embedded distributed inference [94]. All plan statically or per request.

Sun et al.'s LOIP (arXiv 2025–2026), in the library, is the paper that treats **repeated weight loading as a first-class latency term** [95]. On unified-memory Jetsons where weights do not fit, LOIP streams offloaded layers from SSD each decoding step and hides the load behind compute and communication, with a per-step cost T_total = T_comp + communication + #Stage·max_i max(load_i/#Stage − overlap window, 0). A threshold-constrained DP chooses the minimum number of offloaded layers per stage, refined at block level; a UMA-aware loader uses a single pinned buffer filled by io_uring; an online planner offloads one more attention or MLP block at each KV-cache token threshold, so "the switching overhead is bounded by a single block"; and a bandwidth-sensitive KV-parking rule reduces transfer volume immediately when bandwidth drops but **defers upward adjustments unless the next threshold is imminent, with a fluctuation threshold filtering minor changes**, a hysteresis-like rule [95]. On Xavier NX and AGX Orin boards with LLaMA2-13B, Qwen3-32B and LLaMA3.3-70B, it reports up to 8.8× (sporadic) and 20.3× (bursty) speed-ups over pipeline and tensor-parallel baselines, while EdgeShard and Galaxy run out of memory; ablations attribute 26.5% of sporadic latency to the scheduler and 46.8% to UMA loading [95]. LOIP explicitly avoids reallocating layers at runtime because it "would incur excessive overhead", which is implicit support for pricing reallocation, but it never quantifies when replanning would pay. It has no churn, failure handling or phones, and its CUDA kernels do not transfer to ONNX Runtime Mobile.

Kafetzis, Khalili and Koutsopoulos (arXiv 2025), in the library, partition a single decoder layer at attention-head granularity, re-solving every interval of tokens [65]. It is the only library paper whose per-interval objective adds a **migration delay** to inference delay, but that delay is only state transfer, m_i(τ−1)/R_jk(τ), covering KV footprint over link bandwidth, with no load or initialisation cost. Anti-thrashing is a cap of one migration per head per interval. In a custom simulator (25 devices with 2–8 GB and 5–50 GFLOPS, 1–10 Gbps links), the heuristic comes within 15–20% of an exhaustive optimum on small instances and stays under 200 s at the final token where EdgeShard exceeds 1000 s; the headline "up to 9–10×" is not directly traceable to a stated figure value [65]. It is simulation-only, single-layer and churn-free. Its position for CROWDio is "migration-aware, but migration equals state transfer only".

Borzunov et al.'s Petals (ACL 2023 demos), in the library, is the closest analogue to CROWDio's volunteer setting [56], [96]. Servers host contiguous transformer blocks, announce them in a DHT, and a joining server chooses the contiguous interval with the worst throughput, intervals being "always contiguous, since splitting it would harm the inference latency". Clients route through servers by beam search over measured latency, and **store their past inputs to each server so that a failed server can be replaced and its attention cache rebuilt by replay**. All nodes periodically check whether rebalancing "would significantly improve the overall throughput" and switch layers if so. With 8-bit weights halving BLOOM-176B's node count from 44 to 22, Petals reaches 0.83 steps/s over 14 real consumer-GPU servers across two continents, about 10× faster than RAM offloading for single-batch inference [56]. Its rebalancing threshold is qualitative and does not price the time a server spends loading new blocks; servers download their own weights with no peer weight fetch; routing is client-mediated; and it targets GPUs of at least 8 GB. CROWDio's J(new) + margin < J(current) rule with an explicit unload + fetch + load + init term is a quantitative version of Petals' rule, and its checkpoint/resume and drain differ from replay-based recovery.

### 4.4 On-phone collaborative inference: three systems, three different answers to churn

Only a handful of systems run collaborative inference on real Android phones. Chen et al.'s CrowdNet (*IEEE TMC*, 2025), in the library, is the closest neighbour in setting: phones, a crowd, D2D links, churn, ONNX on Android, and BERT-family workloads [97]. It takes the opposite design route to CROWDio. Instead of partitioning a pretrained model losslessly, it trains small "CellNets" that can run standalone or be assembled in arbitrary topologies through transition and aggregation layers, with an A2C policy sampling the connectivity mask; on disruption, detected by heartbeats, the policy reconfigures the topology. Accuracy stays within about 2% of vanilla models on most tasks (SST-2 90.1 versus 91.3 for DistilBERT; CIFAR-100 71.4 versus 71.85 for ResNet-110), and under failure probability 0.8 CrowdNet reaches 84.3% on SST-2, 5.54 points above ensembles and 10.9 above progressive inference [97]. On six Android devices carried by student volunteers walking in an office building, SST-2 latency at batch 1 is 1.66–3.18 s depending on network, and **topology reconfiguration after a failure takes 0.12 s**, which the authors describe as mitigating "the cold inference problem" [97]. That 0.12 s is a policy inference over CellNets that are already resident; CrowdNet never measures loading a new model, reports no energy despite citing energy depletion as a failure mode, requires task-specific cloud retraining, and admits higher communication cost than single-model inference [97]. Its per-device latency is a useful external reference for CROWDio's five-phone DistilBERT pipeline, with the caveat that architectures and measurement differ.

LinguaLinked splits BLOOM models of 1.1–3 B parameters across three Pixel 7 Pro phones and one CUBOT X30 using ONNX Runtime's C++ API, places segments by linear programming, sends activations device-to-device in a ring that never touches the server, and rebalances by overlapping "movable" sub-modules, with reloads of 0.234 s (int8) to 2.449 s (fp32) per balancing event; it has no failure or churn handling and assumes trusted, co-located, always-on devices [98]. Confidant splits LLM fine-tuning across commodity phones by pipeline-parallel training [99]. CROWDio's published subsystem [2] is the third answer: a lossless three-cell DistilBERT pipeline with JIT loading and single residency on five phones, whose 48 s cold and 6 s warm loads are an order of magnitude above LinguaLinked's reload times, suggesting that download, Chaquopy start-up and session creation, rather than raw weight I/O, dominate CROWDio's cold path; this decomposition has not yet been measured.

## 5. The mobile worker: memory, cold start, transport, energy and Python on Android

### 5.1 The memory wall motivates single residency

Commodity phones in CROWDio's evaluation have 3.3–7.4 GB of RAM [2]. The memory wall on devices has been attacked from several directions. Melon combines recomputation and micro-batching with a tensor-lifetime-aware memory pool for on-device *training*, reporting up to 4.33× larger batches, 1.89× average throughput and up to 49.43% energy savings [100]; it is not a layer-offloading or inference system, contrary to its description in [2] (Appendix A). Flash-offloading LLM systems keep weights in storage and load on demand: LLM in a flash uses windowing and row–column bundling to run models up to twice DRAM size [101], and PowerInfer-2 pipelines neuron-cluster I/O with compute on OnePlus phones for models up to 47 B parameters, 3.94× faster than LLM in a flash on average [102]. Hermes overlaps layer loading with execution under a memory budget on a single host, reporting up to 4.24× faster inference and 86.7% lower memory than PipeSwitch for BERT and ViT [103], extended as Hermes+ [104]. LOIP explicitly names "weights stay resident" as the flawed common assumption of Galaxy, Jupiter and EdgeShard [95]. These works support CROWDio's single-residency design, but all assume a fixed device set and fast local storage or interconnect; none distributes shards across volunteer phones.

### 5.2 Cold start is mostly initialisation, not bytes

Two measurement studies anchor CROWDio's switch-cost model. NNV12 (MobiSys 2023) shows that cold inference (read + transform + execute) on vanilla mobile engines is 1.5–12.7× slower than warm inference on CPU and 85.5–443.5× on GPU (the paper gives both 433.5× and 443.5×); for ResNet-50 on a Pixel 5 CPU, weight reading takes only 36.52 ms of a 1363.23 ms cold run while weight transformation takes 1135.28 ms, against 185.82 ms warm [105]. It optimises the cold path with kernel selection, caching post-transformed weights on disk and pipelined execution, for up to 15.2× (CPU) and 401.5× (GPU) speed-ups over prior engines [105]. Kabakibo, Trivedi and Wang's "Breaking the Ice" (MLSys 2026), in the library, decomposes vLLM start-up on H100 and L40S servers into six mostly CPU-bound steps (framework bootstrap, tokenizer initialisation, model loading, torch.compile, KV-cache profiling, CUDA-graph capture), totalling 20.32 s for Llama3.2-3B; **weight loading is only about 7–10% of start-up**, cold SSD reads change total start-up by only about 1.04×, and each step scales near-linearly with a simple parameter, so a per-step linear regression predicts total start-up with errors around 2 s (the paper labels this "MSE" although it is reported in seconds) [106]. GPU upgrades barely help, CPU choice matters more, and vLLM versions vary start-up by more than 4×. The study excludes containers, remote storage and edge runtimes, and is hardware-specific [106].

Together these works argue that "bytes to load" is a poor proxy for switch cost and that initialisation should be measured and modelled separately. CROWDio's decomposition of a switch into unload, conditional fetch and write, load and session initialisation follows the same logic for ONNX Runtime Mobile, and the project should report its own breakdown of the 48 s cold path (download, Chaquopy interpreter and import start-up, ORT session creation and graph optimisation). NNV12's idea of caching post-transformed weights maps directly onto storing ORT-optimised models in CROWDio's shard cache. MLPerf Mobile [107], AI Benchmark [108] and Almeida et al.'s characterisation of 16k+ Play Store apps [109] report steady-state latency and energy; no peer-reviewed benchmark compares ONNX Runtime Android, TFLite, MNN, NCNN and ExecuTorch on session-creation latency.

### 5.3 Activation transport: lossless zlib is an unexplored point between raw tensors and learned bottlenecks

Split-computing work compresses intermediate features with learned, lossy bottlenecks: BottleFit reports 77.1% compression with at most 0.6% accuracy loss on ImageNet and up to 49% lower power than local computing [110], within the taxonomy of [75]. Tile- and overlap-based systems (DeepThings, CoEdge, PICO) reduce transfer by partitioning rather than compression [78], [82], [83], and LinguaLinked sends raw activations with a residual skip transfer [98]. CROWDio instead sends self-describing JSON with base64-encoded, zlib-compressed tensors, redirecting payloads above 1 MB to a shared file store, and reports 62 ± 4% compression on [1, 768] FP32 tensors [2]. Because base64 inflates payloads by a third before compression and RFC 6455 supports binary frames [111], binary frames with fp16 or int8 activation quantisation are a low-risk improvement; learned bottlenecks would alter the model and break CROWDio's exact-equivalence guarantee. The zlib CPU cost on the critical path is acknowledged in [2] but not quantified, and no published measurement of zlib ratios on transformer hidden states was found.

### 5.4 Energy, thermal and Android constraints are first-order but rarely studied for crowds

Sustained inference on phone CPUs triggers thermal throttling with sharp latency increases; Play It Cool mitigates this by shifting among sub-networks of a dynamic network [112], OODIn detects throttling on a Samsung A71 within about 0.8–1.15 s and migrates execution to another engine [113], thermal-aware NPU scheduling and joint heat-and-memory constraints have been studied for single devices [114], [115], and MELT benchmarks transformer energy and thermal behaviour on devices [116]. Android Doze restricts network access to maintenance windows and App Standby buckets limit jobs [117], and app developers experience unexpected behaviours after battery-optimisation API changes [118]. Academic study of these constraints for crowd computing workloads is essentially absent; CROWDio's foreground service with battery and thermal telemetry, and its published 50 ± 3 mAh per run [2], add to a thin evidence base. None of the seven partitioning papers in the library measures energy on phones during partitioned inference.

Two library papers bear on energy-aware control on a single device. Fang, Yang and Wang propose a context-aware multimodal controller that decides per window which modality encoders to run, at what precision (INT4/INT8/FP16) and with which fusion mode, using an MLP of under 0.18 M parameters with **hysteresis over consecutive windows** and two-rate control (context every window; precision and fusion only after stable windows) [119]. On a Jetson Nano and a Raspberry Pi 5 it reports energy falling from 1.04 J to 0.60 J (−42.3%), latency from 112 ms to 52 ms and 2.3× throughput at 0.8% accuracy loss, with controller overhead under 9 ms and 0.32 W; ablations attribute the largest share of savings to modality sparsity. It appears only as a Research Square preprint without confirmed authorship metadata or DOI, reports no confidence intervals or run counts, aggregates three heterogeneous workloads into one table, and models rather than measures its "72% longer battery life" [119]. Its consecutive-window hysteresis and fast-telemetry/slow-reconfiguration split are independent support for damping reconfiguration on edge hardware, and its battery-dependent activation threshold parallels Honeybee-DR's battery tiers [16]. Choudhary et al. (*ITM Web of Conferences*, 2025) present a broad edge-AI framework spanning compression, edge–cloud offloading by bandwidth, load and latency, blockchain-recorded federated updates, differential privacy, RL energy policies and spiking networks, and report 40 ms versus 120 ms latency, 94% accuracy, 1.5 W power and a "67% improvement" in security resilience on Jetson Nano and Raspberry Pi 4 [120]. No workloads, datasets, baselines, run counts or algorithms are given, tables are misnumbered, a reference "[42]" is cited from a list of 15, one reference is "Anonymous (2024)", the conclusion speaks of "the chapter" and "as of October 2023", and several reference DOIs look like placeholders [120]. It can support only general motivation (latency, privacy and bandwidth arguments for on-device inference); none of its numbers should be quoted as comparators.

### 5.5 Python on Android has no academic baseline

Chaquopy embeds CPython in Android apps and ships ARM wheels for packages such as NumPy [121], and ONNX Runtime provides the inference engine [122]. No peer-reviewed treatment of Chaquopy, Kivy, BeeWare or Pyodide for distributed computing or DNN execution on Android was found. CROWDio's use of Chaquopy (codebase and worker, not described in [1]) is therefore both a documentable novelty and a likely contributor to cold-start overhead; reporting interpreter start-up and import cost as a component of the 48 s cold load would be a small but original measurement contribution.

## 6. Reconfiguration and switching cost: every ingredient has a precedent, the combination does not

CROWDio's planner (codebase-only) treats a plan as a choice of cut points and an ordered assignment of stages to phones. Under single residency, moving a stage to a phone costs unload of the current session, a fetch and write of the shard if it is absent from disk (from the server or a peer donor), a load and a session initialisation. The planner searches cut × placement exhaustively or by a Pareto dynamic program over latency, throughput and switch cost, and a controller switches voluntarily only if J(new) + margin < J(current), the advantage persists over several evaluations, a cooldown has elapsed, a per-window switch cap is not exceeded, and the telemetry behind the estimate is not stale. Voluntary switches drain in-flight work; forced switches (worker loss) resume from a retained stage-boundary activation when the cut is kept. Standby-disk prefetch, peer shard fetch and a direct phone-to-phone activation path shrink the fetch and relay components. This section reviews each ingredient's precedents.

### 6.1 Repartitioning downtime was measured early; deciding whether to repartition was left open

Majeed, Kilpatrick, Spence and Varghese's NEUKONFIG (IC2E 2021) is the earliest work found that measures repartitioning downtime for split DNNs [123]. On a Raspberry Pi 3B+ device with x86 edge and cloud servers, VGG-19 and MobileNetV2 in Docker containers over ZeroMQ, a baseline pause-and-resume repartition costs about **6 s of downtime** regardless of CPU, memory or network conditions. "Dynamic Switching" reduces this mechanically: an always-running redundant pipeline cuts downtime below 1 ms at twice the memory (1526.2 MB versus 763.1 MB), while initialising a new pipeline only on change costs about 1.9 s in a new container or 0.6 s inside the existing one, and the old pipeline keeps serving during the switch [123]. NEUKONFIG chooses partitions by latency alone and, in the authors' words, "repartitions DNN whenever there is a change in network speed", which "may adversely impact the performance efficiency"; its future work is to "consider how frequently the DNN must be repartitioned" [123]. It therefore pre-empts the observation that repartitioning has a measurable cost and the make-before-break idea that CROWDio's standby prefetch resembles, and it explicitly names CROWDio's open problem. Its redundant-pipeline approach is infeasible under single residency, which is precisely why CROWDio must price the switch rather than hide it.

CONTINUER, by the same group (IEEE EDGE 2022), chooses among repartitioning, early exit and skip connections when an edge node fails, using a weighted sum of min–max-normalised accuracy, latency and downtime with XGBoost and LightGBM predictors [124]. Its downtime values are empirical constants per technique (decision time plus reconnection, at most 16.82 ms for selection and 16.16 ms for MobileNetV2 repartitioning), not per-candidate reload costs; it covers only forced switches, has no hysteresis or voluntary replanning, redeploys the whole DNN after failure and runs on x86 [124]. AdaMEC (*ACM TOSN*) identifies the cost of "re-partition and re-compilation, storage, re-transmission, and re-offloading" as the core problem and avoids it structurally by pre-partitioning a model once into operator-level "atoms"; when hard constraints are violated it searches combinations outward from the current one and orders the offloading of atoms by a shortest path weighted by atom transmission latency [125]. Transfer cost therefore shapes the migration *order*, not the decision to switch, and load, session-initialisation and residency costs are not modelled. AdaMEC is the closest structural analogue to CROWDio's pre-exported shard catalogue plus fetch, and it reports up to 62.14% latency reduction and 55.21% memory savings on Raspberry Pi, smartwatch and Jetson devices [125].

Several works that appear to address switching do not. Tian et al.'s "server-switching delays" [126], as characterised by the Hao survey [86], are base-station handovers along a known route, with the model "embedded in the mobile device and edge clouds in advance". Luger's TU Wien thesis on "cost-aware" splitting and dynamic rescheduling means monetary AWS cost; the whole model is stored on both sides ("all possible models have to be stored along with all layers"), and rescheduling is triggered every two minutes or on drastic change, which the author places outside the algorithm's scope [127]. Deng et al. (arXiv 2026) re-evaluate a three-tier Raspberry Pi/laptop/desktop partition every 100 inferences and switch only if the new candidate is at least 3% better or a deadline is violated, reducing latency by 6.34–22.92% and energy by 27–36% against static splitting, but model no reload or weight-transfer cost and use no persistence [128]. Deng et al. are the most recent "margin rule" analogue; CROWDio's break-even rule includes the switch cost inside J and adds persistence, cooldown, a cap and stale-estimate refusal. (The full text of Deng et al. was read by one research pass and only the abstract by another; the 3% margin should be re-checked before citation.) Yan, Bi and Zhang combine model placement with online, per-request model splitting for device-edge co-inference [129], conceptually close to CROWDio's slow placement and fast execution. DRL-based partitioners such as DPTO [130] and DDPQN [131] optimise delay and energy; no switching term was found in DPTO, and DDPQN's content was not checked.

### 6.2 Pricing the switch inside the planner: IONN, Huang et al. and the Koch line pre-empt key pieces

Jeong et al.'s IONN (SoCC 2018) is the earliest work to put **model upload cost inside the partitioning graph** [74]. Motivated by a measurement that uploading AlexNet over roughly 80 Mbps Wi-Fi takes about 24 s while local execution takes 1.3 s per query, IONN weights execution-graph edges by layer execution time plus layer upload time, repeatedly takes the shortest path, uploads the newly server-side layers, decays upload time by a factor K < 1 and repeats, so queries benefit before the whole model arrives [74]. It models first-time installation on a server with ample resources, not switching between configurations; there is no load or session-initialisation term, no eviction, no residency limit and no controller. CROWDio's switch term generalises IONN's in the other direction: worker-side shard installation on memory-limited phones, with unload, conditional fetch and write, load and initialisation.

Huang, Zhang and Xu (arXiv:2410.01857, 2024) are the strongest **conceptual** pre-emption [132]. Their motivation states that "switching devices for collaborative edge inference necessitates reloading and setting up the DNN models in the device memory, which introduces overhead … Thus, frequent device and path switching is undesirable." They formulate joint path and partition selection as an adversarial group linear bandit with switching costs, and their B-EXPUCB algorithm uses a block structure to limit switches with an O(T^{1/3}) switching-cost bound. Evaluation is simulation-only and the switching penalty is abstract [132]. A search snippet attributes the same idea to an *IEEE TMC* paper (vol. 25, no. 2, February 2026), likely the journal version, but the record could not be retrieved and is unverified. After this paper, CROWDio cannot claim to be first to recognise reload cost as a switching penalty in collaborative partitioning; it can claim a measured, decomposed, residency-aware cost inside a cut × placement search on real phones.

Koch, Djuhera and Binotto's orchestration paper (arXiv 2025), in the library, and its companion by Djuhera, Koch and Binotto (arXiv:2512.01039) pre-empt CROWDio's trigger design [66], [133]. Both describe an orchestrator with monitoring and capacity profiling, an adaptive orchestrator that keeps the split, redistributes sub-splits or fully re-splits, split revision, and a reconfiguration broadcast that pushes "on-the-fly-generated weight shards and graph rewiring commands". Reconfiguration triggers when EWMA latency exceeds 150 ms, utilisation exceeds 0.85 or minimum link bandwidth falls below 50 Mbps, and "reconfigurations are further rate-limited by T_cool [30 s] to prevent thrashing"; migration is tried before re-splitting [66], [133]. The objective Φ = αL + βU + γP (latency, imbalance, privacy) and the selection d̂ = argmin C(d) contain **no reconfiguration-cost term**: any mapping with lower cost is accepted without subtracting the cost of getting there. Koch et al. is a position paper derived from an IBM patent, with no implementation; its "expected results" (for example 500–1000 ms versus 100–300 ms latency and 5–10 versus 0–2 downtime incidents per hour) are compiled from prior studies, and Djuhera et al.'s KPIs come from an analytical ETSI MEC model [66], [133]. These numbers must not be cited as measured evidence. After these papers, EWMA triggering and cooldown are not novel in themselves; CROWDio's remaining differentiators are the cost-aware margin comparison, persistence, a switch cap, stale-estimate refusal and measured evaluation.

Qiu et al.'s CoCaR (arXiv 2025–2026) puts model loading time into edge-server caching decisions with measured load times (0.877 s to load a ViT sub-model from storage versus 0.248 s to switch between sub-models) and observes that prior work uses load time "only to refine solutions rather than to inform caching decisions"; it targets base-station servers in simulation [134]. Kafetzis et al. price only state transfer [65], LOIP prices per-step weight loading within a fixed plan [95], and Petals rebalances when it "would significantly improve" throughput without a cost term [56].

### 6.3 Serving systems schedule around load cost but never choose a cut

Datacenter serving treats model load and switch latency as first-class, either eliminating it or scheduling around it. PipeSwitch time-shares one GPU among DL applications by pipelining layer-group transfer over PCIe with execution, using unified memory management and active-standby workers, for task start-up overheads of 3.6–6.6 ms [135]; it minimises the cost of a switch but does not decide whether to switch, and it is a single-GPU system, not a multi-device one (Appendix A). Clockwork makes model LOAD to GPU memory an explicit, predicted, centrally scheduled action and treats GPU memory as a model cache [136], the closest datacenter analogue to "residency state in the planner". AlpaServe places partitioned models across GPUs to absorb bursts by simulator-guided search but does not price re-placement [137]. ServerlessLLM uses multi-tier loading-optimised checkpoints, live migration and locality-aware scheduling that picks the server minimising start-up time given where checkpoints already reside, for 10–200× lower latency [138], directly analogous to CROWDio's "fetch only if absent" and best-donor fetch. BlitzScale multicasts parameters GPU-to-GPU from instances already holding them and lets overloaded instances offload layers to instances still loading, cutting tail latency by up to 94% relative to ServerlessLLM [139]; λScale uses RDMA multicast with "execute-while-load" for up to 5× lower tail latency and 31.3% lower cost [140]; and DeepServe (formerly DeepFlow) uses pre-warmed pods, DRAM pre-loading and NPU-fork [141]. Tetris shares tensors among colocated serverless instances for up to 93% memory savings [142], SwapAdvisor searches swap schedules within a single job [143], INFaaS selects model variants with awareness of load latency and of which variants are loaded [144], Tangram reuses GPU memory with affinity [145], and a survey covers serverless inference cold start [146]. Kabakibo et al.'s decomposition (Section 5.2) completes the picture [106].

This literature validates that load and switch cost matter and are predictable, and that peer parameter fetch and locality-aware placement work. It means CROWDio must not claim to be "first to consider model loading cost". None of these systems, however, couples load cost with layer-cut selection on bandwidth-variable mobile devices, and all assume datacenter interconnects.

### 6.4 Elastic and preemptible systems price migration, but are triggered by preemption

Reconfiguration with explicit migration cost is established in preemptible and elastic training and serving. SpotServe re-parallelises LLM serving as spot instances come and go, formulates migration as bipartite matching solved by Kuhn–Munkres to minimise communication, and recovers inference state at token granularity, improving P99 latency 2.4–9.1× [147]; it is the strongest prior for reconfiguration that minimises migration cost and resumes from retained state, the analogue of CROWDio's forced switch. Parcae proactively chooses parallel configurations that maximise "liveput" under predicted preemptions, accounting for migration between configurations [148]. Oobleck precomputes pipeline templates for fast reconfiguration after failure [149], similar in spirit to CROWDio's exhaustive oracle and plan cache; Bamboo uses redundant computation in pipeline bubbles [150]; Varuna morphs jobs on spot VMs with checkpoint-based reconfiguration [151]; Pollux re-allocates resources for goodput and penalises re-allocation to avoid thrashing [152], a scalarised switch-penalty precedent; Gandiva and Optimus measured migration and scaling overheads in early DL cluster schedulers [153], [154]; and Llumnix live-migrates requests and KV cache across serving instances [155]. At the edge, Asteroid's fault-tolerant pipeline replay for collaborative training recovers 14× faster than baselines after device exit [156]. These systems make "reconfiguration with migration cost" not novel in general. CROWDio's distinct position is that its switch cost is dominated by per-device residency transitions rather than network state transfer, that its telemetry is noisy and stale, and that it must decide *whether* to reconfigure voluntarily under bandwidth drift, not only how to recover from preemption.

### 6.5 Online algorithms with switching costs give the break-even rule a theory

The problem of paying a hitting cost plus a switching cost is classical. Metrical task systems admit a (2n−1)-competitive deterministic algorithm for n states [157], and CROWDio's discrete plan space with plan-to-plan switch costs is exactly an MTS. Ski rental gives the 2-competitive break-even rule "switch once cumulative excess cost reaches the switch cost" [158], which CROWDio's persistence requirement approximates. Smoothed online convex optimisation emerged from dynamic right-sizing of data centres [159], [160], was extended to high dimensions by online balanced descent [161], and has variants with predictions [162] and unreliable predictions [163], the latter relevant to CROWDio's stale-estimate refusal. At the edge, Wang et al. model service migration as an MDP with explicit migration cost and threshold policies [164], and Ouyang, Zhou and Chen use Lyapunov optimisation with a long-term migration budget [165]; both place whole services rather than DNN shards with cut selection. Huang et al.'s blocked bandit [132] is the only DNN-partitioning work found with a switching-cost regret bound. No work applies MTS, ski-rental or SOCO analysis with a residency-dependent switch cost to DNN cut and placement selection, and stating even a simple competitive bound for the persistence-plus-break-even rule on two plans would pre-empt the criticism that CROWDio's margin, persistence and cooldown are ad hoc.

### 6.6 Prefetch and peer distribution exist in datacenters and edge caches, not on phones

Edge model caching places models on storage-limited servers to maximise hit ratio within download-latency requirements, exploiting shared parameters (TrimCaching) [166], and CoCaR jointly caches models and routes requests [134]. Peer parameter fetch exists in datacenter form in BlitzScale and λScale [139], [140], and swarm hosting in Petals [56], whose servers nevertheless download their own weights. IONN's incremental upload ordering is a form of prefetch ordering [74], and NEUKONFIG's redundant pipeline is make-before-break [123]. No paper was found on phone-to-phone DNN weight distribution for inference; CROWDio's standby-disk prefetch and best-of-server-or-peer donor term inside the placement objective are new in this setting.

### 6.7 The pipeline throughput model is standard; the switch-cost dimension is not

CROWDio's pipeline model, with a finish-time recurrence, steady-state period equal to the maximum stage compute or communication time, and T_total(N) = fill + (N − 1)·period, is standard: GPipe's micro-batch pipeline [167], PipeDream's DP partitioner minimising the slowest stage [168], PipeEdge [84], EdgeShard [88] and Alpa's inter-operator DP [169]. The planner's DP over layer compositions and device orders must be presented as building on these. The potentially novel elements are the exact Pareto front over latency, throughput and switch cost, the residency and cached-on-disk state carried in the DP, and uncertainty bounds from EWMA variance. Edgent and JALAD are two-objective (latency and accuracy) [68], [71]; no DNN partitioning paper computing an exact Pareto front with a reconfiguration-cost objective was found, although a targeted search of the accelerator-mapping literature was not run and remains a reviewer risk.

## 7. SDKs and programming models: cluster-style APIs on volunteer phones

### 7.1 Distributed Python frameworks assume trusted, homogeneous workers

Python already offers mature task APIs. Ray unifies stateless tasks and stateful actors behind a decorator with futures [41]; Dask provides dynamic task graphs and `map`/`submit` futures [170]; PyWren ships cloudpickled functions to stateless cloud functions for a `map` over AWS Lambda [171], continued by Lithops with a `concurrent.futures`-like API across clouds [172]; Parsl uses decorators returning futures with pluggable executors, scaling beyond 250,000 workers [173]; Fiber reproduces the `multiprocessing` API on clusters [174]; Celery provides decorator tasks with retries over a broker [175]; and the standard library's `concurrent.futures` defines the idiom they imitate [176]. All assume trusted nodes with full CPython and reliable networks. Mobile crowd frameworks (Hyrax, Misco, Honeybee, CWC, BOINC) target phones but expose Java, Android or native-code APIs, BOINC's being a low-level C/C++ checkpoint and progress API over pre-registered binaries [5], [6], [7], [9], [13]. CROWDio's published single-annotation SDK [1] places a cluster-style API on volunteer-phone infrastructure, which is its main SDK novelty; the `crowdio_map` and `crowdio_pipeline` names and the Chaquopy-based worker are codebase-only. No usability study (SUS scores, lines of code) of Ray, Dask or MCC SDKs was found, so the "developer-friendly" claim currently lacks a user study.

Shipping serialised code to volunteers raises a security question the literature does not answer for Android. Python's documentation warns that unpickling can execute arbitrary code [177], and 2024 vendor advisories (CVE-2024-37052 to 37057, CVSS 8.8) show `cloudpickle.load` on a malicious model artefact executing code in MLflow [178]. In CROWDio the trust direction is reversed: volunteers execute code that job submitters send. Submitter authentication, allow-listing or isolation of shipped Python under Chaquopy should at least be discussed.

### 7.2 Model-less serving established objective-driven APIs; CROWDio extends them to partitions

Clipper introduced a model-abstraction layer with adaptive batching against latency SLOs [179]; Nexus bin-packs models onto GPUs with query-level SLOs [180]; Clockwork drops requests that cannot meet deadlines and returns predictable timings [136]; and INFaaS made "model-less" serving explicit, with developers stating performance and accuracy goals and the system choosing variant and hardware, for 1.3× higher throughput, 1.6× fewer SLO violations and up to 21.6× cost savings [144]. KServe is declarative about deployment but not about per-request objectives [181]. Neurosurgeon and Edgent are the classic "the system picks the cut" precedents [67], [68]. CROWDio's codebase-only `submit_job(model_id, inputs, objective, deadline_ms)`, in which the system chooses both the partition cut and the volunteer workers and returns `plan_epoch` and timings, extends the model-less idea to split inference over volatile phones. Exposing the plan epoch per request is unusual in serving APIs and supports reproducibility under reconfiguration.

### 7.3 Partitioning toolchains cut graphs but do not verify or catalogue shards

ONNX's `extract_model` and `Extractor` cut a sub-model between named tensors, with the caveat that boundaries must not cross If/Loop sub-graphs and models over 2 GB use external data [182]; Polygraphy's `surgeon extract` and similar tools do the same [183]; a 3GPP study notes that single-branch splitting is straightforward while multi-branch graphs need cross-cut analysis [184]; and torch.fx symbolic tracing underpins `torch.distributed.pipelining` stage splitting [185], [186]. PipeEdge and EdgeShard partition with custom PyTorch code and report "no accuracy loss" from end-to-end metrics, not per-shard numerical equivalence [84], [88]. Integrity patterns come from safetensors and hub checksums [187] and model registries such as MLflow [188], whose pickle flavours were the subject of the advisories above. The Hao survey's toolchain tables list no artefact pipeline [86]. No tool found pre-exports every contiguous layer-range shard, verifies chained shard output numerically against the source model, enforces a storage budget and emits a sha256 manifest; CROWDio's codebase-only exporter (with a max |diff| < 10⁻⁴ check) does all four. Exporting all O(L²) contiguous ranges rather than only the chosen cut is what lets the planner replan without re-export, which links the SDK contribution to the planner.

### 7.4 Checkpoint APIs: CROWDio sits between system-level and framework-level

Checkpointing ranges from transparent system-level tools (DMTCP [51], CRIU [52]), unavailable to ordinary Android apps, to framework-level checkpointing for tensors (PyTorch DCP with asynchronous save [189], CheckFreq [48], Check-N-Run's differential checkpoints [49]) and application-driven APIs such as BOINC's `boinc_checkpoint_completed` [13]. CROWDio's `@task(checkpoint=True)` style, with source-level instrumentation and base/delta/compacted tiers [1], is as declarative as Ray or Parsl retries, finer-grained than task re-execution, and compatible with Android where CRIU and DMTCP are not. No published application-level checkpointing system for Python on Android was found.

### 7.5 Federated learning SDKs are the mature "Python server, phone client" comparators

Flower is framework-agnostic with Android/TFLite clients [190]; FedML supports on-device training on Android and IoT [191]; PySyft targets privacy-preserving learning [192]; TensorFlow Federated is simulation-focused [193]; and Google's production system selects only eligible devices (charging, idle, unmetered), over-selects to absorb dropouts and paces participation by diurnal patterns [194]. Their client API is "implement fit/evaluate" with code pre-installed on the device, whereas CROWDio ships arbitrary functions and model shards on demand. Eligibility gating, over-provisioning and pace steering are lessons CROWDio can adopt using telemetry it already collects. Transport alternatives to CROWDio's JSON payloads include Arrow IPC [195] and gRPC/protobuf [196].

## 9. Research gaps and CROWDio's positioning: what is pre-empted and what remains

### 9.1 What the literature, including CROWDio's own preprints, already pre-empts

An honest positioning must concede five pre-emptions. First, **reload as a switching penalty**: Huang, Zhang and Xu state that switching devices "necessitates reloading and setting up the DNN models in the device memory" and penalise switches with a regret-bounded blocked bandit [132]. Second, **EWMA-triggered reconfiguration with a cooldown against thrashing**: Koch et al. and Djuhera et al. specify EWMA latency, utilisation and bandwidth triggers with a 30 s cooldown and a migrate-before-re-split order [66], [133]. Third, **fetch cost inside the partition graph**: IONN puts model upload time into the partitioning graph [74], and ServerlessLLM puts checkpoint locality into placement [138]. Fourth, **measured repartitioning downtime and make-before-break**: NEUKONFIG measures about 6 s of pause-and-resume downtime and hides it with a redundant pipeline [123], and PipeSwitch, BlitzScale and λScale hide load latency in datacenters [135], [139], [140]. Fifth, and most important for authorship integrity, **CROWDio's own published results**: the 48 ± 4 s cold and 6 ± 1 s warm loads, single-partition residency, JIT loading, the four-tier affinity scheduler, zlib transport and 1:1 streaming are already public in arXiv:2605.20723 [2], and the single-annotation SDK, tiered checkpointing and entropy-weighted MCDM scheduler are public in arXiv:2604.19363 [1]. A planner paper must cite these as prior work, present the 48 s/6 s figures as previously reported, and frame itself as the reconfiguration-aware extension; [2] itself names "memory-adaptive multi-partition residency" and "aggressive pre-fetching" as natural extensions. Further partial pre-emptions include LOIP's deferred-upward bandwidth rule as hysteresis [95], Deng et al.'s improvement margin [128], SpotServe's resume from retained state [147], Petals' volunteer block rebalancing [56], and PipeEdge/EdgeShard/PipeDream's DP over layers and devices [84], [88], [168].

### 9.2 What remains novel

No counter-example was found for the following conjunction, which is the defensible novelty of the planner and its supporting SDK and worker features. (i) A **decomposed, residency-dependent switch cost** (unload, conditional fetch and write from the best server or peer donor, load, session initialisation) evaluated per (shard, device) under a hard single-residency constraint on phones, and grounded in measurements on real handsets. (ii) That cost embedded **inside** the cut × placement search (exhaustive oracle, exact Pareto DP over latency, throughput and switch cost, or scalarised DP), rather than handled by a separate loading layer or added after the fact. (iii) A **voluntary-switch controller** that combines a break-even margin on cost-inclusive objectives with persistence, cooldown, a per-window switch cap and refusal to switch on stale estimates, driven by EWMA means and variances with sample ages and counts; Koch/Djuhera have the cooldown but no cost term, Huang has the cost but no telemetry gating, and Deng has a margin but no cost. (iv) **Epoch semantics** that drain in-flight work on voluntary switches and resume from a retained stage-boundary activation on forced switches. (v) **Phone-side standby-disk prefetch, peer shard fetch and direct phone-to-phone activations** as alternatives to server relay, which no surveyed phone system offers together. (vi) **Lossless, verified shard export** of every contiguous layer range with a numerical-equivalence check and sha256 manifest, which enables replanning without re-export. (vii) On the backend, a **transfer-time cost model with uncached-artifact, max-min fair Wi-Fi share and Sufferage mapping**, and a **simulator that runs the production scheduler code** against a virtual fleet. The novelty is compositional and empirical; the claim should be phrased as "among the surveyed works" and backed by the ratio of measured switch cost to measured gain on real phones, which is the empirical core.

### 9.3 Gaps CROWDio itself leaves open

The review also identifies gaps that a thesis or paper should either close or acknowledge. On evidence: no published decomposition of the 48 s cold path into download, Chaquopy start-up and ORT session creation, despite NNV12 and Kabakibo showing why it matters [105], [106]; no ablation isolating each mechanism; no baseline beyond CROWDio's barrier mode; no network-bandwidth sweep; scale limited to five or six devices; and planner results not yet published. On theory: no competitive or regret bound for the break-even plus persistence rule, which MTS and ski-rental make easy to state for two plans [157], [158]. On scheduling: no churn or availability prediction [30], no deadline-based admission control [31], no result validation (Honeybee-DR's trap jobs are a ready model [16]), no speculative replication [53], [55], and no battery- or thermal-tiered eligibility of the kind used in federated learning [194] and Honeybee-DR. On scope: linear chains only, whereas DAG-shaped models would need min-cut formulations [70], [64]; encoder-only DistilBERT, whereas decoder LLMs add KV-cache dynamics [95], [65]. On security: no treatment of executing shipped Python from untrusted submitters on volunteer phones [177], [178]. On usability: no user study of the SDK. And on evaluation methodology, the switch-cost-aware planner should be compared against a ladder of baselines that isolate each ingredient (static plan; greedy replanning without switch cost; a fixed-margin rule in the style of Deng et al.; load-time awareness without residency state, in the style of Clockwork or INFaaS; and asynchronous make-before-break switching in the style of NEUKONFIG or PipeSwitch), and its controller overhead should be measured separately, as Fang et al. do [119].

## 10. Conclusion

The literature has converged on two facts that it has not yet combined. Systems work on serving, elastic training and LLM offloading has shown that loading a model is expensive, decomposable and predictable, and theory has long known how to trade a hitting cost against a switching cost. Partitioning work, meanwhile, has built ever more capable planners for heterogeneous edge devices while assuming either that every node holds the whole model or that the plan never changes. On volunteer phones neither assumption survives: memory forces single residency, churn forces replanning, and the switch costs seconds to tens of seconds, comparable to the gains being chased. CROWDio's contribution is best understood not as the discovery of any single mechanism (reload penalties, cooldowns, upload-aware partitioning and phone pipelines all have precedents, some of them CROWDio's own) but as the first measured system in which the cost of changing a partition is a first-class, residency-dependent term inside the planner that decides the partition.

That framing has practical consequences for the thesis. It makes the ratio of switch cost to replanning gain, measured on real handsets and decomposed by component, the central empirical result; it makes the cited prior art a strength rather than a threat, because each pre-emption isolates one ingredient that CROWDio composes; and it demands that the published record be cleaned up first, since several of the systems claims in the current workshop paper rest on misattributed or unverifiable references.
